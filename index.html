Card 01 · A quiet year, briefly loud

The city went quiet, then erupted.

Two stories braided into one year: a six-week stay-at-home trough and a three-day spike of unrest at the end of May.

A reading of 8.5 million records · Chicago Data Portal

IThe city goes quiet

On March 21, 2020, Illinois ordered residents to stay at home. By the end of the next week, the daily count of reported incidents in Chicago had fallen well below the typical level. For the six weeks of the order, the city averaged · incidents a day, against · a day in the same calendar window across 2018 and 2019. A drop of ·.

The mechanism is the same one that runs through the blizzards card. Routine activity needs people in the street: bus stops, parking lots, sidewalks, bars. Pull them indoors and most of what the city counts as crime quietly idles. Narcotics enforcement collapses hardest because it depends almost entirely on stops in public places. Theft, burglary, criminal damage all drop too, by smaller fractions.

The chart below holds the whole year at a glance. The dark fill across April and May is the lockdown trough. The brief red column rising out of it at the end of May is something else. A different story hidden inside the same year. Press play to look closer.

IIUnrest after George Floyd's death

A spike inside the trough

Eight days in late May

May 25 → June 5, 2020. A brief surge in burglary, criminal damage and weapons charges sits inside the six weeks of lockdown.

George Floyd was killed by police in Minneapolis on May 25, 2020. Protests started there and reached every major American city within a week. Chicago's downtown marches escalated on the evening of May 30, and the city raised its bridges over the river. By the end of May 31 the daily incident count had reached ·, the highest single-day total of the year and about · above the surrounding two-week baseline. A 9 pm city-wide curfew began the next day. By June 3 the line was back inside the lockdown trough.

A note on what the data does and does not show

Tens of thousands of people marched in Chicago and across the country in the days after George Floyd's death. Those protests were lawful and overwhelmingly peaceful. The chart shows a coincident rise in specific kinds of crime (burglary, criminal damage, weapons violations) during the same period of unrest. That is a correlation in time, not proof that the protests caused the crime, and protesting is not what is captured here.

The chart above held the spike against a year of mostly empty streets. The chart below shows what was inside the spike. Burglary jumps from a typical 264 per week to 1,069 in eight days, the largest move on the page. Criminal damage rises about seventy percent, from roughly 655 to 1,115. Weapons violations and public peace offences climb sharply too, from small baselines. Everyday street crime, like theft and narcotics, falls hard during the same days, because the city was otherwise still under stay-at-home and the rest of the street had gone even quieter than baseline.

IIIEach type's own year

Step back from May for a moment. Each crime category has its own shape across 2020: different magnitudes, different timing. Some collapse and stay low through summer. Some recover by autumn. Some barely move. The small multiples below put the eight robust types side by side, on the same weekly x-axis, against the 2018-19 baseline. The lockdown band and the Floyd week are drawn across every panel so the two reference events line up.

The year is two stories braided into one chart. The dominant one is structural: a six-week ratchet downward when the city's routine activity stops. The brief one is reactive: a three-day spike of property crime and disorder when the streets fill again, this time for unrest. Both are real. Neither would be visible without the other.

Crime in this dataset is mostly what happens when people are out. Empty the streets and the count falls. Fill them with something other than ordinary life and the count rises, briefly, in shapes the chart could not have predicted in advance.

Card 02 · A regime change in policing

After Laquan McDonald, the arrest line broke.

Across the mid-2010s, the monthly volume of reported incidents in Chicago barely moved. The share of those incidents that ended in an arrest fell off a cliff, and never recovered.

A reading of 8.5 million records · Chicago Data Portal

IHow the case unfolded

On the night of October 20, 2014, an officer named Jason Van Dyke fired sixteen rounds at seventeen-year-old Laquan McDonald on South Pulaski Road. A dashcam in a nearby cruiser recorded the whole thing. For thirteen months the city kept the file out of public view.

A freelance journalist named Brandon Smith spent most of 2015 trying to pry the file loose, first under the Illinois Freedom of Information Act and then through Cook County court. The city's lawyers fought him at every step, arguing that release would prejudice an open investigation. On November 19, 2015, Judge Franklin Valderrama ordered the city to produce the video within five days. Hours before that deadline expired on November 24, the Cook County State's Attorney charged Van Dyke with first-degree murder. The video (six minutes long, almost silent) went out over the wires the same evening.

The release detonated a week of demonstrations. Crowds shut down Michigan Avenue on Black Friday and returned downtown through most of December. Mayor Rahm Emanuel asked for and accepted the resignation of Superintendent Garry McCarthy on December 1. Within days, the head of the city's police-oversight body had also stepped aside; the Cook County State's Attorney was facing public calls to do the same. For a stretch of weeks, the city operated in open political crisis.

The case was a scandal; the city went through a year of political fire over it. The question is whether anything actually changed in how the Chicago Police Department does its job.

IIThe arrest rate broke

Yes, and sharply. The crime did not change. Across the 48 months centred on November 2015, the monthly volume of reported incidents drifts by only ·. The response did. In the 24 months before the marker, roughly · of incidents ended in an arrest. In the 24 months after, ·. A gap of · opened in about a year, and the line settled into a new groove well below the old one. Same incidents. Different department.

Why the response changed is harder to say. The most-discussed explanation (debated among critics, researchers, and inside the department for years afterward) is what some call de-policing, or the Ferguson effect. The mechanism is plausible. Proof is harder. A second factor lands inside the same window: an ACLU stop-report agreement, signed in August 2015, that added paperwork to every investigative street stop and reshaped how those encounters were recorded. The chart cannot separate these threads from each other. It can show when the line broke, not which thread broke it.

The break was not a single shock. It was a cascade that ran for years, through City Hall, the police department, and the federal government.

The institutional cascade

Each event below is dated from the public record. They are presented as context for the data shift, not as a chain of causes.

    IIICase to decree

    The case did not end there. The DOJ probe opened on December 7 became, by January 2017, a 164-page report finding a pattern of excessive force and constitutional violations by the Chicago Police Department. When the incoming Trump administration declined to pursue reforms, Illinois Attorney General Lisa Madigan sued the city in August 2017 to force them anyway. That suit produced a settlement and, on January 31, 2019, a federal consent decree.

    Scandals fade. The next question is whether this one did. Was the break a brief reaction, the kind of dip that follows political fire and then resets, or did the new level hold?

    Consent decree

    A court-enforced reform agreement. A federal judge oversees compliance, an independent monitor reports on it, and the department is required to revise its practices on use of force, training, and accountability.

    IVIt held

    It held. A decade on, the line has not climbed back. The arrest rate fell off a cliff in late 2015 and stayed down. A step, not a dip.

    It is tempting to read the decree onto this picture: the reform that kept the new level in place. The dates make that hard. The drop began in late 2015. The decree was entered in January 2019, more than three years later. Whatever cemented the change was cemented before the decree existed. Reading any post-2019 decree effect is harder still: the pandemic arrives in 2020 and disrupts every series in Chicago's records for the years that follow. What the timing on this chart points to is the case itself (the moment something visible happened inside the department), not the legal mechanism that arrived years later. That is not a verdict on whether the decree worked. It is a reading of when the line moved.

    The data on this page fixes the when, sharply. The why is contested, and this article does not try to settle it. Any of these threads (the case itself, de-policing, the stop-report paperwork, federal scrutiny) can be pulled. None of them can be claimed.

    Ten years on

    Several institutional changes followed. In 2016 the city replaced its police-oversight body with the Civilian Office of Police Accountability, or COPA. A separate policy, adopted the same year, requires the city to release video and other records from police shootings and similar serious incidents within sixty days.

    Card 03 · When the city shuts down

    When winter shuts Chicago down, the streets empty.

    Three of the harshest winter events in a quarter-century of Chicago's incident records. Two were polar vortexes. One was a blizzard. The same answer each time.

    A reading of 8.5 million records · Chicago Data Portal

    IThree winters, the same drop

    On February 2, 2011, twenty inches of snow fell on Chicago. Drifts buried Lake Shore Drive and the city stopped. That day, the police logged 320 incidents, less than a third of the usual weekly average.

    The same drop showed up in January 2014, when O'Hare hit minus twenty-three Celsius for three straight mornings, and in January 2019, when the polar vortex pushed wind chills below minus thirty. Three winters, three mayors, three police chiefs. Each time the streets went quiet and the count fell.

    The chart below puts all three side by side. It's the first thing to say about Chicago and extreme winter.

    IIWhy the count falls

    Cohen and Felson's 1979 routine activity theory of crime asks for three things to be in the same place at the same time: a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of anyone who could intervene. The streets are where they meet. Sidewalks, bus stops, parking lots, bars. Most of what the city counts as crime starts there.

    Extreme winter empties those streets. People stay in. They don't wait at bus stops. They don't get into fights on corners. They don't break into garages because the garages are buried under snow. All of that requires people in the street. When nobody is in the street, the count stops. That can happen because of cold (2014, 2019) or because of snow (2011). The calendar in the margin shows that February 2011 wasn't especially cold for a Chicago winter. It was, however, impassable, and crime fell harder than in either polar vortex.

    The chart below pairs temperature with crime, day by day. Pick an event, press play, and watch the dot move down and to the left as the air gets colder, then climb back as the city thaws. The line follows the order of the dates. It's not a fitted curve. The day-to-day relationship between temperature and crime is not clean, and we do not draw it that way.

    IIIWhat goes, what holds

    Not every kind of crime drops by the same amount. The outdoor categories fall hardest: narcotics deals, theft, the stops that turn into other charges. Battery and assault, which often happen between people who already live together or stay indoors together, hold up better.

    IVAfter the city reopens

    What about afterwards? Does the count snap back when the city reopens? Does it overshoot, as people make up for the days they lost? Or does it just rejoin the seasonal floor?

    The trough is short in all three events. Within a few days each line is back within ten percent of its baseline. After that, the answers diverge. The 2011 and 2014 series settle quietly along the dashed baseline for the rest of the window. The 2019 series climbs back through the baseline and stays above it for ten straight days, running ten to forty percent ahead. That is one overshoot in three events. It is real in the numbers, but n=1 is not a pattern, and we do not present it as one.

    Jan 2014 · O'Hare temperature

    Crime in the dataset is mostly opportunity. Opportunity needs contact, and contact needs people outside. Take the outside away and the count falls. Take it away in three different winters across two decades and it falls the same way each time.

    Nothing the police department or the courts have tried in twenty-five years produces a drop on the scale of what twenty inches of snow or a thirty-below wind chill does in forty-eight hours. The window is short and the mechanism is involuntary. This is not a policy lesson. For forty-eight hours, the weather does what no department could.
